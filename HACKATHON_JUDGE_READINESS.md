# ReadQuest — Hackathon Judge Readiness

This document turns the judging rubric into a practical plan. Scores below are mock-panel estimates based on the current repository and stated concept, not official results or guarantees.

## Researched problem definition

**Sharper problem definition:** ReadQuest should target upper-elementary and middle-grade learners who can read many of the words on a page but struggle to build meaning from the passage—especially to explain an inference and point to the sentence that supports it. The educator problem to validate is that answer-only practice may show a wrong answer without making clear whether the learner misunderstood the question, formed a weak inference, or chose irrelevant evidence. IES guidance distinguishes decoding from comprehension needs and supports explicit vocabulary/comprehension routines for grades 4–9; ReadQuest tests whether brief modeled practice plus evidence-choice feedback helps tutors see what to teach next. This is a product hypothesis, not a proven classroom pain or demonstrated ReadQuest effect. [IES/WWC Grades 4–9 practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/29).

The broader need is significant: in 2024, 40% of U.S. fourth-graders scored below NAEP Basic. That share was higher than in 2022 but not significantly different from 1992. NAEP levels are trial standards and not equivalent to state grade-level proficiency, so use the result as context, not as a count of potential customers. One released grade-four item about selecting highlighted text to support an idea received full credit from 12% of students; that single item is illustrative, not a population estimate of evidence skill. IES gives strong evidence to comprehension-building practices for struggling readers in grades 4–9. It supports the instructional mechanism, but does not prove ReadQuest effective. [NAEP grade-four results and sample items](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g4_8/national-trends/?grade=4) · [NAEP reporting guidance](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g4_8/about/reporting/) · [IES/WWC Grades 4–9 practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/29).

**Device framing:** describe device distraction as a possible context or contributor, not the proven cause of falling scores. Position ReadQuest as purposeful, short-duration learning on a device that helps learners slow down and explain their reasoning. OECD findings associate some forms of device distraction/leisure use with poorer outcomes; findings on educational use are more nuanced. [OECD PISA 2025 findings](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html).

**Important dosage limit:** the IES evidence supports sustained, explicitly taught comprehension practice. Many underlying interventions lasted weeks, and many delivered frequent 40–60 minute sessions. A five-minute ReadQuest is therefore best pitched as a supplemental practice tool that reinforces teacher-led instruction—not a proven stand-alone reading intervention. [IES practice-guide evidence details](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC-practice-guide-reading-intervention-full-text.pdf).

## Product thesis: gamified comprehension

ReadQuest is a story-driven reading-comprehension game for learners who need reasons to keep practicing. The learning action should move the game forward: the learner reads a passage, makes an inference, chooses the sentence that supports it, and sees how that reasoning changes the quest. A guide character, chapter progress, collectibles, and responsive feedback create anticipation and return motivation around that learning loop.

The product hypothesis is that this structure earns more voluntary repeat practice while preserving or improving the quality of comprehension practice. Measure those outcomes separately: return rate and completed quests show engagement; accuracy, evidence choices, and an appropriate skill measure show learning signals. XP should reward completion and careful reasoning, never speed or streak pressure. Sound, motion, and character animation should support the loop and remain optional.

Duolingo demonstrates the power of playful, bite-sized learning as a product pattern. That pattern is now common in literacy too, so “gamified” is a product direction rather than a market differentiator by itself. ReadQuest must make the learner’s reasoning visible and give a tutor a useful next step, then prove that both children and paying adults value the experience.

## Best initial audience and customer

- **Long-term learner audience:** early readers through junior high, with age- and reading-level-appropriate content.
- **Best first learner segment:** grades 4–8 students who can decode a passage but need support with comprehension, vocabulary, inference, and citing evidence. This aligns with IES intervention guidance for grades 4–9 without pretending one experience fits every age.
- **Best first pilot and payer to test:** an independent literacy tutor or small reading-tutoring practice serving grades 4–8. This lets the team observe the child’s game experience and test whether the adult values the assignment and follow-up summary. A teacher/interventionist remains a valuable instructional pilot partner; their budget control may be limited.
- **Next buyers:** after-school literacy programs and tutoring centers can expand seat volume; schools offer broader reach after the product proves value and clears privacy, security, procurement, rostering, and support requirements.
- **Parent role:** gatekeeper and possible later payer for a family plan. A consumer subscription depends on strong child retention and parent-perceived value against free alternatives; test it after the tutor workflow.
- **Market ceiling, not TAM:** NCES lists about 18.1 million public-school students in grades 4–8 in fall 2022 (sum of grade-level enrollment). Not all need this product; this is not a serviceable market or revenue forecast. Size a real market from intervention-group counts in pilot districts. [NCES enrollment table](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_203.40.asp).

## What ReadQuest should prove in the demo

Make the demo a story quest where reading and reasoning change what happens. Briefly model an inference, show how to find its supporting sentence, then give the learner three focused attempts. The tutor summary should preserve the learner’s answer and evidence choice. The key moment is the learner seeing how a strong or weak clue changes the quest, then comparing their choice with the correct evidence and explanation. This matches IES guidance to model comprehension routines, provide question practice, and use text evidence; it does not prove ReadQuest’s effectiveness. Competitors also provide text questions and evidence-based work. [IES/WWC, Recommendation 3 Part B](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC-practice-guide-reading-intervention-full-text.pdf).

Make character, sound, story, and rewards part of the learning loop: evidence-based reasoning should move the quest forward. Keep sound and motion optional, and use rewards to recognize completion and careful thought. A systematic review of gamification in primary education found generally positive engagement/motivation but mixed learning results, so ReadQuest must measure learner return separately from comprehension outcomes. [Primary education gamification review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883035524001666).

**Wedge to test:** short, narrative quests for grades 4–8 that make inference and text evidence the action that advances the story. Before feedback, the learner selects the supporting sentence. Afterward, the character explains why that evidence fits, and the tutor sees the answer, evidence choice, target skill, and next practice in one quick summary. This gives ReadQuest a more focused job than a broad language-arts game, but competitors may already cover parts of it. Compare the exact workflow with tutors and learners before claiming differentiation.

**What could become hard to copy:** a reviewed library of compelling age-appropriate quests tagged to specific comprehension skills; a feedback design that helps learners repair reasoning; teacher/tutor reports that save review time; and evidence that the game produces repeat use without trading away learning quality. Characters, XP, music, and illustrated worlds alone are easy to copy.

**Positioning statement to test:** “ReadQuest turns comprehension practice into story quests. Learners advance by explaining what the text supports; tutors see the reasoning and the next skill to practice.” Avoid leading with “kids are distracted by phones.” The market problem is the need for engaging, useful comprehension practice; screen distraction is contextual research, not the proven root cause.

### Audience, user, buyer, and first channel

- **Core learner:** grades 4–8 students who can read a passage but need practice making an inference, identifying the gist, or supporting an answer with text evidence.
- **Daily user:** the child, who needs an enjoyable repeatable game loop and age-appropriate reading content.
- **First buyer to validate:** independent literacy tutors and small reading-tutoring practices that assign repeat practice and communicate progress to families. They may make purchasing decisions faster than districts, but willingness to pay and software budget are unproven.
- **First pilot partner:** a tutor or interventionist who can observe whether the quest supports instruction and whether the summary saves time.
- **Later buyers:** after-school literacy programs and schools. These can provide larger deployments; they add approval, privacy, procurement, rostering, support, and evidence requirements.
- **Parent role:** gatekeeper and likely payer for a later family subscription. Test this path only after learners return to the game and parents see a reason to choose it over free options.

This focus replaces the vague “early readers through junior high” launch audience. Keep broader age coverage as the long-term vision; build and test one grade band first. The grade 4–8 focus fits the comprehension need and IES intervention guidance for grades 4–9.

### 4. Impact and growth — target 4/5

- Pilot with one tutor-led or teacher-led group and track active learners, quests completed, evidence choices, skill-level accuracy, repeat use (including week-to-week return), and minutes spent preparing/reviewing each session.
- Report engagement and learning signals separately. Track first-quest completion, next-quest selection, week-one return, and sessions per active learner alongside accuracy on the target skill, evidence-selection quality, and a short age-appropriate transfer item. More time in the app is not, by itself, learning impact.
- Test whether characters, narrative progress, and rewards increase voluntary return versus a minimal plain-practice version. Keep quest content and question difficulty comparable; this can support an engagement design decision, not a broad efficacy claim.
- For an initial pilot, report reach, completion, evidence use, skill-level accuracy, and teacher feedback. A short pilot can establish usability and engagement, not broad or durable reading gains.
- Before the pilot, define one narrow skill outcome (for example, selecting text evidence for an inference), use equivalent pre/post items that are not the same demo questions, and record the starting point. Report the number invited, consented, active, completing, and lost to follow-up; include dates, session count, tutor/teacher time, and all results, including null or negative findings.
- Treat a small pilot as usability and feasibility evidence. To claim ReadQuest improves comprehension, use a larger, adequately designed evaluation with a comparison condition where feasible, age-appropriate measures, and transparent limitations; do not infer durable gains from quest accuracy or a simple pre/post change alone.
- **Growth gates:** (1) one tutor/teacher confirms the workflow and uses it again; (2) several independent tutors pay and renew, with support time and contribution margin measured; (3) a small practice adopts it across tutors; (4) only then test school-wide sales, which require privacy/security review, procurement, rostering, reporting, and evidence of learning value. Add early-reader and junior-high versions only after each age band gets content and usability review.

## Two-day hackathon plan for a 3.5 overall

The weekend goal is a convincing prototype and credible early market evidence. Paid renewals, a powered learning study, school procurement, and a complete market count belong after the event.

One weighted profile that reaches **3.5/5** is Tech 3, Problem 4, Market 4, Impact 3, Presentation 4. This requires a complete working quest and a clear pitch; research alone cannot reach it. The market score can be stronger at the concept stage if the team has direct buyer conversations and clear assumptions, but do not present interest as a sale.

### Before judging: prioritize these deliverables

1. **One complete playable quest:** one original passage, one target skill, a short worked example, three questions, learner-selected evidence, passage-grounded feedback, story consequence, progress/reward, and a tutor summary. Keep the demo local and reliable.
2. **One vivid game moment:** a clue choice visibly changes the story or unlocks the next scene. Use one guide character, one small animation, and one optional sound cue. Leave extra worlds, a big content library, accounts, AI, multiplayer, and complex backend work out of the two-day build.
3. **Three fast discovery conversations if reachable:** talk to a reading tutor, interventionist/teacher, and parent or learner. Ask what they use today, what is frustrating, and what would make them try the quest again. Show the working demo and record exact reactions. Label this as directional feedback.
4. **A one-slide competitor and business case:** compare Prodigy English, Reading Eggspress, and ReadTheory; state the specific grade 4–8 evidence-choice/tutor-summary wedge; show the $29/month tutor-account scenario and what it costs to support. Label assumptions and identify why a buyer might choose it.
5. **A measured pilot request:** ask two or three tutors/interventionists to try the quest after the event. A scheduled follow-up or pilot commitment is useful early evidence. Do not imply a paid contract or efficacy result without one.
6. **A practiced 2–3 minute pitch:** problem → learner → live game decision → tutor value → market and price assumption → pilot ask. Rehearse with a stopwatch and a reset demo.

### A realistic score ladder

- **Market 3 at the event:** specific learner and buyer, credible competitor comparison, researched broad market context, transparent price/cost assumptions, and a plausible first channel.
- **Market 4 at the event:** all of the above plus several real conversations that confirm the pain or workflow, evidence that the learner game is compelling, and at least one concrete pilot follow-up. Paid conversion is not a reasonable two-day requirement.
- **Market 4 after the event:** run the planned pilot, show repeat use, collect paid continuation decisions, measure support/acquisition costs, and build a filtered local list of eligible literacy providers.

This keeps the weekend pitch ambitious while protecting credibility. Judges can reward a well-researched, testable business case without hearing a fabricated claim of traction.

### Evidence needed to earn each 4

- **Problem 4:** Use the NAEP trend and IES practice guide accurately; have educators/tutors confirm the specific unmet task; demonstrate a modeled answer-and-evidence routine followed by learner practice and useful feedback.
- **Market 4 after the hackathon:** Complete at least 10 discovery interviews, including at least 5 likely payers; document alternatives, budget owner, and objections; observe at least 3 workflows; compare with Prodigy English, Reading Eggspress, ReadTheory, and free materials; secure at least 3 pilot commitments and test at least 2 paid continuation decisions after people have used it. Show learner return and a filtered reachable beachhead. This is directional discovery, not a representative market survey.
- **Impact 4:** Run a documented pilot with educator consent, age-appropriate safeguards, a defined skill measure, baseline/follow-up, usage, attrition, and transparent limitations. Do not substitute XP, time-on-app, or satisfaction for learning evidence.
- **Presentation 4:** Deliver a practiced 2–3 minute story, show a deterministic working quest and evidence reveal, state the customer and economics, and end with a specific pilot request.
- **Technical 4:** Ship the core flow, persistent progress, evidence accuracy, keyboard/mobile use, privacy-safe handling, and reliable offline demo. This is currently the largest gap and cannot be raised by better research alone.

### Customer-discovery questions to use

Ask tutors, school educators, and parents separately; do not lead with the product pitch. For likely payers, observe a real preparation/review workflow when possible.

1. What does the learner currently do when they can read the words but cannot explain what a passage means?
2. How do you notice whether the learner understands the text, and what evidence do you see?
3. What tools or routines do you use now? What is frustrating or missing?
4. How often does this problem arise, and what happens if it is not addressed?
5. Who chooses and pays for a new practice tool? What approval, privacy, or device requirements apply?
6. After showing the prototype: what would make you use it next week, stop using it, or recommend it?
7. After trying the prototype, would you commit to a paid continuation at $29/month per tutor account for up to 40 learners (an unvalidated scenario)? What would you expect that account to include, and what would make the price unacceptable?

Record exact answers, role, grade band, existing tool, and next-step commitment. Count pilot commitments separately from compliments or hypothetical purchase interest.

### Pitch draft for a working demo

> In 2024, 40% of fourth-graders scored below NAEP Basic. That signals a serious reading challenge; it does not establish a cause or prove which product will solve it. ReadQuest turns comprehension practice into story quests for grades 4–8. A learner moves the story forward by making an inference and choosing the sentence that supports it. The character’s response makes the reasoning memorable, while the tutor sees the answer, evidence choice, and next skill to practice. Strong products already gamify reading, so we are testing a focused question: will learners return to this evidence-based quest, and will tutors find its follow-up view useful enough to keep paying for it? Research supports explicit comprehension practice for struggling readers in grades 4–9; it does not yet prove ReadQuest works. We’re seeking three tutors or interventionists for a pilot measuring completion, evidence choices, skill accuracy, week-to-week return, and tutor time.

Keep the demo claim honest: say “we are seeking pilot partners” until a pilot is actually underway. [NAEP](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g4_8/national-trends/?grade=4) · [IES/WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/29).

## Go / no-go before presenting

- [ ] A judge can finish one quest in under three minutes.
- [ ] The evidence reveal highlights the correct sentence for every demo question.
- [ ] No key button or card is decorative; all visible controls work.
- [ ] The team can name its first pilot grade band, likely buyer, and one clear next customer conversation.
- [ ] Pricing, cost, and impact claims are labeled as measured facts or assumptions.
- [ ] The demo works with sound off, reduced motion, and no internet connection.
